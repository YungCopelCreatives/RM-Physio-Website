Understanding Plantar Fasciitis

Learn about this common cause of heel pain and how physiotherapy can help

Plantar fasciitis is the most common cause of heel pain, affecting millions of people worldwide. The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue that runs along the bottom of your foot, connecting your heel bone to your toes. When this tissue becomes inflamed or develops small tears, it causes the characteristic sharp, stabbing pain typically felt with the first steps in the morning or after periods of rest.

Key Facts About Plantar Fasciitis

  • Affects approximately 10% of the population at some point in their lives
  • Most commonly occurs between ages 40-60, but can affect anyone
  • Often related to biomechanical factors and overuse
  • Conservative physiotherapy treatment is successful in 90% of cases
  • Early intervention typically leads to faster recovery
  • Left untreated, can become chronic and more difficult to resolve

Symptoms & Causes

Recognizing plantar fasciitis and understanding contributing factors

⚡

Common Symptoms

How to recognize if you have plantar fasciitis

▼

Primary Symptoms:

  • Morning Pain: Sharp, stabbing heel pain with first steps after waking
  • Post-Rest Pain: Pain after sitting or resting for extended periods
  • Activity Pain: Discomfort during or after walking, running, or standing
  • Tenderness: Pain when pressing on the inside of the heel

Pain Characteristics:

  • Usually worst in the morning, often improves with movement
  • May worsen again after prolonged activity
  • Can range from mild discomfort to severe, disabling pain
  • Typically affects one foot initially, but can affect both
⚖️

Risk Factors

Factors that increase your likelihood of developing plantar fasciitis

▼

Biomechanical Factors:

  • Flat Feet or High Arches: Altered foot mechanics
  • Overpronation: Excessive inward rolling of the foot
  • Tight Calf Muscles: Limited ankle flexibility
  • Leg Length Discrepancy: Uneven loading patterns

Lifestyle & Activity Factors:

  • Sudden increase in activity level or intensity
  • Prolonged standing or walking on hard surfaces
  • Running, especially on hills or uneven terrain
  • Worn-out or unsupportive footwear
  • Weight gain or obesity
  • Age (most common between 40-60)
🔬

Contributing Conditions

Medical conditions that may increase risk

▼

Associated Conditions:

  • Diabetes: Affects tissue healing and flexibility
  • Arthritis: Joint inflammation and stiffness
  • Thyroid Disorders: Can affect connective tissue
  • Heel Spurs: Often present but not always the cause of pain

Occupational Factors:

  • Jobs requiring prolonged standing (healthcare, retail, manufacturing)
  • Working on concrete or hard surfaces
  • Wearing inadequate footwear for work demands
  • Shift work affecting sleep and recovery patterns
🏃

Athletic Considerations

Sport-specific factors for athletes and active individuals

▼

High-Risk Sports:

  • Running: Especially long-distance and on hard surfaces
  • Tennis: Quick direction changes and jumping
  • Basketball: High impact and frequent jumping
  • Dancing: Repetitive movements and pointed feet positions

Training Errors:

  • Too much, too soon - rapid increases in training volume
  • Inadequate warm-up or cool-down routines
  • Training through pain or ignoring early symptoms
  • Inadequate recovery time between training sessions

Our Evidence-Based Treatment Approach

Comprehensive physiotherapy program designed to eliminate heel pain and prevent recurrence

1

Comprehensive Assessment

Duration: 60 minutes

  • Pain History: Detailed discussion of symptom onset, patterns, and aggravating factors
  • Biomechanical Analysis: Foot posture, gait assessment, and movement patterns
  • Physical Examination: Palpation, range of motion, strength, and flexibility testing
  • Footwear Assessment: Evaluation of current shoes and wear patterns
  • Activity Analysis: Understanding work, sport, and daily activity demands
  • Special Tests: Windlass test, first step pain reproduction, and functional tests
2

Pain Relief Phase

Focus: Reduce inflammation and manage acute pain

Manual Therapy:

  • Soft Tissue Massage: Deep tissue work to calf, plantar fascia, and foot muscles
  • Joint Mobilization: Ankle and foot joints to improve mobility
  • Trigger Point Release: Addressing tight spots in calf and foot muscles
  • Neural Mobilization: Techniques to address nerve tension

Pain Management Strategies:

  • Ice therapy for inflammation control
  • Taping techniques for support and pain relief
  • Activity modification guidelines
  • Night splint recommendations if appropriate
3

Mobility & Flexibility Phase

Focus: Address underlying biomechanical restrictions

Stretching Program:

  • Calf Stretching: Gastrocnemius and soleus muscle stretches
  • Plantar Fascia Stretching: Specific techniques to lengthen the fascia
  • Achilles Tendon Stretching: Addressing posterior chain tightness
  • Hip and Knee Stretches: Addressing compensatory tightness

Mobility Exercises:

  • Ankle range of motion exercises
  • Foot and toe mobility exercises
  • Self-massage techniques using tennis ball or roller
  • Joint mobilisation exercises
4

Strengthening Phase

Focus: Build strength in foot and leg muscles

Foot & Ankle Strengthening:

  • Intrinsic Foot Muscles: Toe curls, marble pickups, short foot exercises
  • Calf Strengthening: Progressive heel raises and eccentric loading
  • Posterior Tibialis: Arch strengthening exercises
  • Plantar Fascia Loading: Graduated loading exercises

Proximal Strengthening:

  • Hip abductor and stabilizer strengthening
  • Core stability exercises
  • Balance and proprioceptive training
  • Functional movement patterns
5

Functional Rehabilitation

Focus: Return to full activity without pain

  • Progressive Loading: Gradual return to walking, running, and sport activities
  • Gait Retraining: Optimizing walking and running mechanics
  • Sport-Specific Training: Activity-specific movements and drills
  • Footwear Guidance: Recommendations for appropriate shoes and orthotics
  • Return-to-Sport Protocol: Structured program for athletes
6

Prevention & Maintenance

  • Long-term Exercise Program: Maintenance exercises to prevent recurrence
  • Biomechanical Correction: Ongoing attention to movement patterns
  • Activity Management: Guidelines for training progression and load management
  • Self-Treatment Skills: Techniques for managing minor flare-ups
  • Follow-up Care: Scheduled check-ins to monitor progress

Self-Care & Prevention Strategies

Evidence-based strategies you can use at home to manage and prevent plantar fasciitis

🏃

Morning Routine

  • ✓ Gentle ankle pumps before getting out of bed
  • ✓ Calf stretches while lying in bed
  • ✓ Foot massage with tennis ball
  • ✓ Gradual weight-bearing on feet
  • ✓ Supportive slippers or shoes immediately
👟

Footwear Guidelines

  • ✓ Good arch support and heel cushioning
  • ✓ Avoid walking barefoot on hard surfaces
  • ✓ Replace worn-out shoes regularly
  • ✓ Consider custom orthotics if needed
  • ✓ Avoid flat shoes and high heels
❄️

Pain Management

  • ✓ Ice bottle roll for 10-15 minutes after activity
  • ✓ Anti-inflammatory medication as advised
  • ✓ Contrast baths (warm/cold water)
  • ✓ Rest from aggravating activities
  • ✓ Elevate feet when resting
💪

Daily Exercises

  • ✓ Calf stretches (3 x 30 seconds, 2-3 times daily)
  • ✓ Plantar fascia stretch with towel
  • ✓ Toe curls and marble pickups
  • ✓ Short foot exercise (arch activation)
  • ✓ Tennis ball massage (morning and evening)

When to Seek Professional Help

  • Heel pain persists for more than 2-3 weeks despite self-care
  • Pain is severe and limiting daily activities
  • Morning pain is not improving with stretching
  • You're unable to walk normally due to pain
  • Pain is getting progressively worse
  • You have numbness or tingling in addition to heel pain

Treatment Success & Recovery Timeline

What to expect from physiotherapy treatment for plantar fasciitis

Typical Recovery Timeline

  • Weeks 1-2: Pain reduction and initial mobility improvements
  • Weeks 3-6: Significant improvement in morning pain and daily function
  • Weeks 6-12: Return to normal activities and exercise
  • 3-6 months: Full recovery and return to all sports/activities

Success Rate: Approximately 90% of plantar fasciitis cases resolve with conservative physiotherapy treatment within 6 months.
